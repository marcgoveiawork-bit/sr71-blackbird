SR-71 Blackbird

Lockheed Skunk Works

Mach 3.3. 85,000 feet.

Thirty-two built. None ever lost to enemy fire. Scroll on to explore the engineering behind the fastest jet ever to fly.

The airframe

Built from titanium

About 85% of the structure is titanium alloy, because aluminium would soften at cruise. The skin passes 250 °C, so panels were made to fit loosely: on the ground the Blackbird leaked fuel, and only sealed up once it was hot.

The design

Drawn by hand

Kelly Johnson's Skunk Works team developed the Blackbird family in the late 1950s and early 1960s, starting with the CIA's A-12. There was no computer-aided design: the shapes behind it were worked out on drawing boards and slide rules.

Exterior tour, 1 of 5

Inlet spikes

Each engine sits behind a movable cone. As speed builds it slides back about 66 cm to hold the shock wave in exactly the right place. At Mach 3.2 the inlet itself makes most of the thrust.

Exterior tour, 2 of 5

Chines

The sharp ridges that run along the nose blend body into wing. They add lift, keep the nose steady at high angles, and scatter radar energy away from the receiver.

Exterior tour, 3 of 5

Two crew, one behind the other

The pilot sits in front, the reconnaissance systems officer behind. Both wore full pressure suits, close relatives of the ones astronauts used.

Exterior tour, 4 of 5

Fins canted 15° inward

Both vertical tails move as one piece and lean inward, which keeps them from reflecting radar straight back like a flat wall.

Exterior tour, 5 of 5

Two Pratt & Whitney J58s

Around 34,000 lbf each with afterburner. At low speed they behave like turbojets; at Mach 3 air bypasses the core and they work much more like ramjets.

Takeoff

Lit by a green flash

JP-7 fuel is so stable you can drop a lit match in it. To start the engines and light the afterburners, the jet injected triethylborane, which ignites on contact with air in a bright green flash.

One SR-71 cost about ten times as much in today's money. Flying it was dearer still: once tankers and ground crews are counted, estimates pass $200,000 an hour, and a bill of $200–300 million a year helped retire the fleet in 1990.

Climb

Up to 85,000 feet

At that height the air is so thin the sky goes dark at midday, and the crew could see the curve of the Earth.

Cruise

Mach 3.2, glowing

Friction heats the leading edges until the whole airframe grows several centimetres longer. The crew rode it out in cooled pressure suits, behind canopy glass too hot to touch.

BLACKBIRD

Designed by Lockheed's Skunk Works and flown by the US Air Force from 1966 to 1998, the SR-71 cruised above Mach 3 at more than 80,000 feet, photographing the ground below and outrunning anything sent to stop it. Two Pratt & Whitney J58 engines, an airframe of titanium, and a speed record that still stands.
